Living with the Seasons

The classics advise changing your habits four times a year — sleep, mood, food and movement all follow the calendar.

四时养生 · sì shí yǎng shēng Reading time: 6 min read
“The sages followed yin and yang, harmonized with the four seasons.” 圣人春夏养阳，秋冬养阴。—— 《黄帝内经》

Spring: rise and grow

Spring belongs to Wood — new shoots, wind, and the Liver network. The classical advice is to sleep slightly later, rise earlier, walk in the fresh morning air, and loosen both body and mind after winter’s contraction. Sour foods are reduced (they "gather" and tighten, against the season’s expansive nature); green vegetables, sprouts and gentle stretching suit the season.

Emotionally, spring is when frustration and irritability surface — the "stuck" feeling. Movement is the medicine: walking, stretching, venting productively rather than suppressing.

Summer and autumn

Summer belongs to Fire and the Heart. Hydration, moderate sunshine, joy and connection are seasonal virtues — but overdoing heat and stimulation depletes fluids and disturbs sleep. Bitter and cooling foods (bitter melon, mung bean soup) are traditional counters to summer heat.

Autumn belongs to Metal and the Lungs — a season of dryness and of letting go. The advice turns to earlier nights, moistening foods (pear, honey, white fungus, lily bulb), and protecting the throat and neck from wind. Grief surfaces naturally now; the season asks for simplifying, decluttering, finishing.

Winter: keep the reserve

Winter belongs to Water and the Kidneys — the season of storage. Sleep more, go to bed earlier and rise later, keep the lower back and feet warm, and eat warm, slow-cooked foods: stews, bone broths, black beans, walnuts and chestnuts. Classical texts say that what you conserve in winter becomes the energy you spend all spring.

Whatever the season, one thread runs through all of it: adjust before the body complains. Seasonal living is prevention as a daily practice — small nudges, four times a year.
