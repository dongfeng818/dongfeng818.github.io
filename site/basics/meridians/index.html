Meridians and Acupuncture Points

The channels that connect everything — the map acupuncturists have used for over two thousand years.

经络 · jīng luò Reading time: 6 min read
“The meridians determine life and death, treat disease and regulate deficiency and excess.” 经脉者，所以能决死生，处百病，调虚实。—— 《黄帝内经》

Rivers through the body

Meridians (jing luo) are the channels along which qi and blood are said to circulate. The twelve primary channels run like rivers, each named for the organ network it serves — for example, the Stomach channel travels from below the eye, down the front of the body, along the leg, and ends at the second toe. Where channels meet and branch, smaller "luo" connections spread like irrigation canals.

Along the channels lie more than 360 classical acupuncture points — places where the flow is thought to be accessible from the surface. Stimulating a point with a fine needle, warmth (moxibustion) or finger pressure (acupressure) is traditionally said to regulate the flow through the connected network.

Why points far away work

The meridian map explains a feature of clinical practice that puzzles newcomers: a needle in the leg for a headache, or pressure on the hand for nausea. If a channel passes through both places, the classical reasoning goes, its flow can be influenced at any point along it — like opening a valve downstream to release pressure upstream.

Modern research on acupuncture is active and evolving: some mechanisms proposed include local release of endorphins, effects on connective tissue, and modulation of pain signalling. Traditional and modern explanations coexist; the map remains the common language of the clinic.

Try it safely

You can explore the meridian map without needles. Gentle acupressure on points such as LI-4 (Hegu, in the web between thumb and index finger — avoid during pregnancy) or PC-6 (Neiguan, two finger-widths above the wrist crease, classically used for nausea) is a safe way to feel the system for yourself.

Follow the body’s comfort: firm but never sharp pressure, circular motions for 30–60 seconds, and stop if anything feels wrong. Curiosity is a fine teacher; pain is not.
