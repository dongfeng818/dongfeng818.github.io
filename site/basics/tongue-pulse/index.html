Reading the Body: Tongue and Pulse Diagnosis

Before laboratories and imaging, Chinese physicians learned to read the body’s living signals — and they still do.

舌脉 · shé mài Reading time: 6 min read
“Look, listen, ask, and feel — the four examinations.” 望闻问切，四诊合参。

The tongue: a living report

In TCM diagnosis, the tongue is read as a real-time display of the interior. Its colour reflects the state of blood and fluids: pale suggests deficiency, deep red suggests heat, purple suggests stasis. The coating — the layer on top — reflects the state of digestion: thick and greasy points to dampness, peeled or absent coating suggests fluids are damaged.

Even the regions of the tongue are mapped: the tip is associated with the Heart, the sides with the Liver, the centre with the Spleen and Stomach, and the root with the Kidneys. A red tip, for example, is classically read alongside restless sleep and a busy mind.

One caveat for daily observers: coffee, curry, antibiotics and orange juice all repaint the tongue within hours. Practitioners ask what you have recently eaten and drunk before drawing conclusions — you should too.

The pulse: more than beats per minute

TCM pulse-taking traditionally reads the radial artery at three positions on each wrist, at two depths, feeling for qualities such as strength, width, tension and rhythm. A "wiry" pulse like a guitar string accompanies stress and Liver qi stagnation; a "slippery" pulse like a pearl rolling appears in pregnancy and damp conditions; a "thin and thready" pulse suggests blood deficiency.

Where Western medicine counts frequency, the classical reading explores texture — a reminder that two people with the same heart rate can feel entirely different to a trained hand.

Four examinations, one picture

Tongue and pulse never stand alone. They belong to the four examinations: looking (complexion, posture, spirit), listening and smelling (voice, breath), asking (the deep interview about sleep, thirst, digestion, emotions) and feeling (pulse and palpation). The pattern that emerges — for example, "Liver qi stagnation turning to heat with yin deficiency" — then guides herbs, acupuncture and lifestyle advice together.

This is the heart of TCM as a systems medicine: the diagnosis names a pattern of the whole person at a moment in time, not a permanent disease label.
